Three long-term schemes used for retirement and other long goals in India. This explains how each works and how long the money is locked in. Interest rates and returns are not covered: the PPF rate is set by the government each quarter, the EPF rate is declared each year, and NPS returns depend on the markets.

## PPF (Public Provident Fund)

- A government savings scheme, opened at a post office or authorised bank.
- Deposits from ₹500 to ₹1,50,000 in each financial year.
- The account matures after 15 complete financial years from the end of the year it was opened, and can be extended in blocks of five years.
- One partial withdrawal a year is allowed after five years from the end of the year of opening; loans against the balance are allowed in the earlier years.
- Closing early is allowed only after five years and only for specified reasons (such as serious illness or higher education), at a lower interest rate.

## EPF (Employees' Provident Fund)

- A retirement fund for salaried employees, run by EPFO.
- Each month the employee contributes 12% of basic pay plus dearness allowance. On ₹30,000 of basic pay and DA, that is ₹3,600. The employer also contributes 12%; part of it goes to the pension scheme (EPS) and the rest to EPF.
- The full balance is paid on retirement, or after a period without employment. Partial withdrawals are allowed for specified needs (such as housing, education or illness) under EPFO's rules, which change from time to time.
- The account moves with the employee when they change jobs, through their UAN.

## NPS (National Pension System)

- A pension scheme regulated by PFRDA, open to most citizens; contributions are invested in equity, corporate bonds and government securities in a chosen mix.
- Tier I is the pension account, with limits on withdrawals before exit. Tier II is an optional account that can be withdrawn from freely.
- On exit at retirement, part of the corpus must buy an annuity (a regular pension) and part can be taken as a lump sum. The shares and thresholds are set by PFRDA's exit rules, which have changed over time; the current rules apply.

## Comparing them

The main differences are who can join, how the money is invested (fixed rate or market-linked), and when it can be taken out. Tax treatment depends on the tax regime and the year, and is not covered here.
