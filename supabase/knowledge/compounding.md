Compounding means earning returns on your earlier returns, not only on the money you first put in. Each year's growth is added to the balance, and the next year's growth is worked out on that larger balance.

## A worked example

You invest ₹1,00,000 and it grows at 8% a year, with the growth left in.

- After 1 year: ₹1,00,000 × 1.08 = ₹1,08,000.
- After 10 years: ₹1,00,000 × 1.08¹⁰ = about ₹2,15,892.
- After 20 years: ₹1,00,000 × 1.08²⁰ = about ₹4,66,096.

With simple interest (8% of the original ₹1,00,000 each year, never added back), 10 years gives ₹1,80,000. The extra ₹35,892 in the compounded case is growth on growth.

Doubling the time from 10 to 20 years more than doubles the gain (₹1,15,892 to ₹3,66,096), because the later years grow a bigger balance. That is why time matters as much as the rate.

## The rule of 72

A quick estimate of how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 8%, 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. The exact figure at 8% is about 9.01 years.

## What changes the result

- The rate, and whether it is steady. Investments whose value moves up and down do not compound at a fixed rate.
- How often growth is added (yearly, quarterly, monthly).
- Costs, taxes and withdrawals, which reduce the balance that keeps growing.
- Inflation, which reduces what the final amount can buy.

The 8% in the example is for illustration only, not an expected or current return.
