Diversification means spreading money across different investments so that a loss in one has a smaller effect on the whole. It can be across asset classes (shares, bonds, gold, deposits), across companies and sectors, and across time.

## A worked example

₹1,00,000 in a single share that falls 40% becomes ₹60,000.

Split instead into four investments of ₹25,000 each, where the same share falls 40% and the other three stay unchanged: ₹25,000 × 0.60 + ₹75,000 = ₹90,000. The same event costs ₹10,000 instead of ₹40,000.

The same split also limits the gain if that one share doubles, which is the trade-off.

## What it cannot do

Diversification reduces risk specific to one company or sector. It does not remove market-wide risk: in a broad fall, most shares fall together. Holding different asset classes, which do not all move together, helps more with that.

## Ways people diversify

- Mutual funds and index funds, which hold many companies in one scheme.
- Holding more than one asset class.
- Investing over time (for example, through an SIP) rather than all at once.

## Over-diversification

Holding many schemes that own the same shares adds paperwork without adding much spread. Checking what each holds shows whether they really differ.
